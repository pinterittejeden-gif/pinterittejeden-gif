Water Receipt

A sealed holder sits in flowing water with separate capsules for different chemicals. Each capsule keeps a permanent DNA record if its target passes by, and the whole thing is collected later. Basically a receipt for whatever the Tuesday sample missed.

Published work shows that biological circuits can preserve evidence of a past exposure in DNA, and that cell-free sensors can detect contaminants in water. The main question is whether those ideas can work together in dirty water for a full week.

the sampling problem

The pollution may have left before the bottle arrived

A grab sample describes one moment. A short discharge at night can disappear downstream before the next visit. Continuous instruments can help, but they are expensive, need power, and do not exist for every chemical worth worrying about.

what is already known

The useful part is the week in dirty water

The project is not claiming to invent DNA memory or cell-free water sensors. It combines them inside a retrievable sampler and asks whether the combination survives the place where it is meant to work.

published starting point

Cells can remember a chemical exposure

A recombinase memory circuit retained a record of analyte exposure for eight days and was also tested in a wastewater Pseudomonas isolate (Kalvapalle et al., 2024).

published starting point

Cell-free sensors work with water samples

ROSALIND detected antibiotics, small molecules, and metals in water without living cells (Jung et al., 2020). CRIBOS later coupled cell-free chemical sensing to recombinase memory on paper (Chen et al., 2025).

unproven connection

Leave the memory reaction submerged for days

Each capsule would expose a protected cell-free reaction to flowing water through a membrane while keeping the reagents contained. A short exposure must leave a stable record that survives until collection.

likely failure

Wastewater eats the clever part

Temperature swings, enzymes, particles, pH, biofilms, and chemical mixtures may destroy the reaction, block diffusion, or switch it on when the target was never there.

cartridge design

One holder, several removable capsules

Each capsule checks one target. A holder could carry capsules for zinc, tetracycline, arsenic, fluoride, atrazine, and the controls, although every target would need its own compatible sensor chemistry. The first experiment should still use one target before adding the others.

Outer case

A boring object that can be recovered

A labelled cage attaches to a drain or flow channel, protects the capsules from debris, and opens without losing them. If retrieval is annoying, nobody will use it twice.

Water window

A membrane between the sewage and the reaction

Each capsule has a membrane that must let its dissolved target diffuse in while retaining DNA, enzymes, and the support material. Several pore sizes and protective layers would need testing because wastewater does enjoy clogging things.

Separate capsules

One chemical per capsule

A zinc capsule and a tetracycline capsule would contain different sensors, even though both use the same basic DNA-memory idea. Arsenic, fluoride, and atrazine could be added only where suitable sensor chemistry exists. Separate control capsules show whether the holder stayed sealed and whether the reactions survived.

Collection

Open it later and check which DNA changed

The first lab version can use PCR to compare changed and unchanged DNA in every capsule. The point is a reliable historical answer, not a glowing screen on a sewer pipe.

first test

Give it one short pulse and six long days

A seven-day flow loop would compare clean water and raw wastewater. Some cartridges receive a thirty-minute target pulse on different days. Others never see the target or see a similar chemical instead.

controls

A remembered event still has to be real

Every deployment needs enough internal controls to tell a true exposure from a dead reaction, a leaky circuit, a clogged membrane, or a cartridge that spent the week having a chemical breakdown.

No-target capsule

Measure false memories

A matched capsule lacks the target sensor but contains the same reaction components. Any DNA change here is background, not pollution.

Always-on capsule

Prove the chemistry survived

A separate reaction should complete regardless of the target. If it fails, silence from the sensing capsules means nothing.

Flow marker

Prove water reached the inside

A harmless tracer or passive dye records wetting and approximate exchange through the membrane without being part of the DNA result.

Reference sampler

Keep an ordinary method nearby

Timed water samples and a conventional passive sampler provide independent measurements. The biological cartridge does not get to grade its own homework.

first experiments

Start in a clean tube, end in ugly water

The sequence separates memory chemistry, membrane transport, storage, and wastewater interference before pretending this belongs in a real drain.

  1. One target first

    Reproduce one cell-free sensor and DNA-memory reaction in a tube using a known target such as tetracycline or zinc.

  2. First capsule

    Immobilise the reaction on paper or hydrogel, seal it behind a membrane, store it dry, rehydrate it, and measure unwanted DNA changes.

  3. Membrane test

    Measure target entry and reagent escape across candidate membranes in clean flowing water.

  4. Seven-day loop

    Deliver brief pulses on different days and read every cartridge at the end of the same deployment.

  5. Add a second capsule

    Run two target capsules and the controls together, then check whether their chemistry leaks, interferes, or produces confusing results.

  6. Wastewater challenge

    Repeat in raw wastewater with temperature changes, fouling, related chemicals, and ordinary reference samples.

  7. Real placement

    Only after containment and controls pass, test a non-reporting cartridge beside routine sampling at one managed facility.

What already works on paper

These papers establish the memory circuit and the cell-free sensing pieces. None reports a continuously submerged, retrievable cell-free memory cartridge in wastewater.
