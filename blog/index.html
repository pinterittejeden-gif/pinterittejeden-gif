how I read

How I study biology for fun

I follow questions, read the surrounding papers, draw diagrams, and stop when the reading stops being useful.

August 2026 about 6 minutes

Biology is what I read outside of work. It covers almost everything, so a question rarely stays small.

I am not studying for an exam and I do not run a laboratory. I want to understand how living things work, especially the parts that are hard to explain.

This is the method I use. It is not optimised and there is no fixed routine.

I start with a question, not a subject

"Learn plant biology" is too large to be useful. "Can a plant move enough copper into wood to matter?" gives me somewhere to begin. A specific question immediately produces smaller ones: how copper enters roots, how plants stop it becoming toxic, where it is transported, and what concentration would actually change the wood.

I start with the simplest version of the question. If I cannot explain it in one sentence, I do not understand it yet.

the basic loop Ask something concrete. Find the missing words. Search again.

I learn enough vocabulary to stop searching badly

The first search is rarely good. I use broad sources to learn the terms people in the field actually use. "Plant moves metal" eventually becomes things like uptake, translocation, xylem loading, sequestration, and hyperaccumulation. The search results become more relevant.

I keep a list of unfamiliar terms and write one plain definition beside each, enough to avoid relearning the same word.

I read papers in passes

I used to read every paper line by line. That is an inefficient way to read.

Now I read the title, abstract, figures, and conclusion first. I am trying to answer three things: what did they actually test, what happened, and does it matter to my question? If it does, I go back through the methods and results. If it does not, I leave.

I do not need to understand every sentence before I am allowed to learn something from a paper.

I keep the evidence separate from my idea

This is the most important part. I write down what a source showed, then separately write what I think might follow from it. Those are not the same thing, even when the connection feels obvious.

For each project I try to keep four things visible: what is established, what I am proposing, what would need to be tested, and what result would make me drop the idea. The last one shows whether the idea can be rejected.

I draw the mechanism

A diagram shows gaps that paragraphs can hide. If I draw a metal entering a root and cannot draw a believable path to the wood, that is useful. The missing step is the useful part.

The drawings are not meant to look scientific. Boxes, arrows, and question marks are enough. Every assumed step is made visible so it can be checked individually.

I follow disagreements

The interesting part often begins when two papers do not fit together. Sometimes they used different species, conditions, measurements, or definitions. Sometimes one result simply does not replicate well. Either way, the disagreement tells me which detail I had been ignoring.

I also read reviews and follow their reference lists.

I stop before it becomes homework

I do not force myself to finish a topic. If a question goes cold, I leave it. Sometimes it returns later with a connection to something else.

The goal is not to collect completed subjects. It is to get better at asking questions, judging evidence, and noticing where I am guessing. I remember more from a problem I care about than from a list.

What I actually keep

  • A question written in normal language
  • A short vocabulary list
  • Links to the papers I actually used
  • One rough mechanism diagram
  • A clear line between evidence and my guess
  • A reason the idea might fail

The project write-ups on this site are the cleaned-up version.
